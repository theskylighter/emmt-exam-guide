**A coil's current creates a magnetic field H; what actually builds up inside, the flux density B, depends on the material, because the material's own dipoles add a magnetization M.** Permeability measures how much the material helps.

## The Problem: Same Coil, Different Results

Wind a coil and pass a fixed current through it. Measure the field inside. Now slide an **iron rod** into the coil and measure again: the field is hundreds or thousands of times stronger, though the current has not changed. We need words and units that separate **what the coil does** from **what the material adds**.

## The Idea in Plain Words

- **H, the magnetic field**: the "push" produced by the current. It depends only on the coil.
- **M, the magnetization**: the extra field from the material's dipoles lining up.
- **B, the inductance or flux density**: the total field inside, coil plus material.

:::analogy
H is how hard you push a crowd; M is how many people in the crowd join in and push the same way; B is the total push you end up with. A lazy crowd (vacuum) adds nothing; an eager crowd (iron) multiplies your effort.
:::

## How It Works

<<SVG:askeland_fig20_2>>

| Relation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $H = \dfrac{nI}{l}$ | field of a coil ($n$ turns, current $I$, length $l$) |
| $B = \mu_0 H$ | flux density in vacuum |
| $B = \mu H = \mu_0 H + \mu_0 M$ | flux density with a material |
| $M = \chi H$ | $\chi$ = magnetic susceptibility |
| $\mu_r = \dfrac{\mu}{\mu_0} = 1 + \chi$ | relative permeability |
| $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \text{H/m}$ | permeability of free space |

| Quantity | Name | SI unit | cgs unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | magnetic field | A/m | oersted (1 A/m = 4 pi x $10^{-3}$ Oe) |
| B | inductance, flux density | tesla (Wb/m2) | gauss (1 T = $10^{4}$ G) |
| M | magnetization | A/m | |
| $\mu$, $\mu_r$ | permeability, relative permeability | H/m, none | |
| $\chi$ | susceptibility | none | |

In Figure 20-2 the material's line has a **steeper slope** than the vacuum line: the slope is the permeability, and the gap between the two lines is **$\mu_0$ M**, the material's contribution.

:::example
**A solenoid (Askeland Example 20-2).** A coil of 10 turns, 1 cm long, carries 10 mA. Then $H = (10 \times 0.01\ \text{A}) / 0.01\ \text{m} =$ **10 A/m**, which is $10 \times 4\pi \times 10^{-3} =$ **0.1257 oersted**. To reach $B = 2000$ gauss the core needs $\mu = 2000 / 0.1257 =$ **about 15,900 gauss/oersted**, a relative permeability of about 15,900, so a high-permeability soft material such as the 4750 alloy is chosen.
:::

:::key
- **H** comes from the current; **M** from the material; **B** is the total.
- **B = $\mu_0$ H + $\mu_0$ M**; **M = $\chi$ H**; **$\mu_r$ = 1 + $\chi$**.
- 1 T = $10^{4}$ G; $\mu_0$ = 4 pi x $10^{-7}$ H/m.
:::

:::mistakes
- **Using B and H as the same thing.** H is the applied push; B includes the material's response.
- **Forgetting the $\mu_0$ in B = $\mu_0$ H + $\mu_0$ M.** Both terms carry $\mu_0$.
- **Mixing units.** In SI, H and M are in A/m and B is in tesla.
:::

:::exam
- Short definitions (*"Define permeability and susceptibility"*) and unit questions.
- The relation B = $\mu_0$(H + M) is needed to explain why **B never saturates** in a hysteresis loop.
:::

:::def
The **magnetic field H** (A/m) is produced by a current ($H = nI/l$ for a coil). The **flux density B** (tesla) is the total field inside a material, $B = \mu H = \mu_0 H + \mu_0 M$, where **M** is the **magnetization** contributed by the material's dipoles, $M = \chi H$. **Permeability** $\mu = B/H$ measures how readily the material supports flux; relative permeability $\mu_r = \mu/\mu_0 = 1 + \chi$.
:::

## Quick Check

:::q Q1 · A material has $\chi$ = 999. What is its relative permeability?
:::ans
$\mu_r$ = 1 + $\chi$ = **1000**.
:::
:::

:::q Q2 · Convert 0.5 T into gauss.
:::ans
1 T = $10^{4}$ G, so 0.5 T = **5000 G**.

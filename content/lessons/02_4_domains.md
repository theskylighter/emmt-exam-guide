**A ferromagnet is divided into small regions called domains; inside each, all moments point the same way, but different domains point in different directions, so an unmagnetised piece shows no net magnetization.**

## The Problem: Why Isn't Every Iron Nail a Magnet?

The exchange interaction lines up the spins of neighbouring iron atoms (see [Where Magnetism Comes From](02_1_magnetic_dipoles.html)). So why does an ordinary iron nail not stick to other nails? And why can you magnetise it by stroking it with a magnet?

## The Story

In **1907**, the French physicist **Pierre Weiss** proposed a bold answer: an "internal (molecular) field" aligns the moments, but only within **small regions**. Each region is fully magnetised, yet the regions point in different directions and cancel. Nobody could see these regions at the time; they were confirmed decades later. They are now called **Weiss domains**.

## How It Works

- **Domain**: a region in which **all the dipoles are aligned** in one direction.
- In a material never exposed to a field, the domains are **randomly oriented**, so the **net magnetization is zero** (Fig 20-5a). This arrangement **minimises the magnetostatic energy**: one big domain would spread a strong field outside the material, which costs energy.
- **Bloch wall**: the narrow boundary between domains, where the moment direction **changes gradually and continuously** from one domain's direction to the next (Fig 20-5b).
- Sizes (Askeland): domains are about **0.005 cm or smaller**; Bloch walls are about **100 nm** thick.
- A domain is **not** the same as a grain: one grain can hold several domains, and a very small grain may hold just one.

### What a field does to domains (preview)

1. Domains already pointing roughly along the field **grow**: their Bloch walls move.
2. Then the remaining domains **rotate** into the field direction.
3. When all are aligned the material is **saturated**.

When the field is removed the walls do not all move back, which is why the material can stay magnetised. That is the whole story of the [hysteresis loop](02_5_hysteresis.html).

:::key
- **Domains**: regions of fully aligned moments; random overall in an unmagnetised sample.
- **Bloch walls**: gradual transition zones (about 100 nm) between domains.
- Domains form to minimise **magnetostatic energy**.
- Magnetising = **wall motion** (growth), then **rotation**.
:::

:::mistakes
- **"Domains are the same as grains."** A grain can contain many domains; grain boundaries are crystal boundaries, Bloch walls are magnetic boundaries.
- **"The direction flips sharply at a Bloch wall."** It rotates **gradually** across the wall.
- **"An unmagnetised ferromagnet has no aligned moments."** Each domain is fully aligned; only the overall sum is zero.
:::

:::def
A **magnetic domain** is a region of a ferromagnetic or ferrimagnetic material in which all the magnetic dipoles are aligned in the same direction. Domains form so as to minimise magnetostatic energy, and in an unmagnetised material they are randomly oriented, giving zero net magnetization. Adjacent domains are separated by **Bloch walls**, narrow zones (about 100 nm) across which the moment direction changes gradually.
:::

## Quick Check

:::q Q1 · Why does a piece of iron form many domains instead of being one big domain?
:::ans
One big domain would create a large external field, which stores a lot of **magnetostatic energy**. Splitting into oppositely oriented domains lowers that energy.
:::
:::

:::q Q2 · What happens to Bloch walls when a small field is first applied?
:::ans
They **move**, so domains aligned with the field **grow** at the expense of the others.
:::
:::

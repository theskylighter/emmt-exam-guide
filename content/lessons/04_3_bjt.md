**A junction transistor is two p-n junctions back to back: a forward-biased junction injects carriers into a very thin base, and almost all of them sweep straight through into the collector, so a small input change controls a large output current.** That is amplification in a crystal.

## The Problem: A Diode Cannot Amplify

A [diode](04_2_diode_working.html) can only *steer* current. To send a phone call across a continent, the weak signal must be **amplified** again and again, and computers need **switches** that one signal can turn on and off. Tubes did both (the triode), but they were hot, fragile and short-lived. Bell Labs needed a **solid-state triode**.

## The Story: Christmas 1947

After the war Bell Labs set up a solid-state team led by **William Shockley**. On **23 December 1947**, **John Bardeen and Walter Brattain** pressed two gold contacts, very close together, onto a germanium crystal and saw **amplification**: the *point-contact transistor*.

Shockley had been left out of that breakthrough. Within weeks (January 1948) he designed a sturdier device built entirely from p-n junctions: the **junction transistor**, which is the version Callister describes. The three shared the **1956 Nobel Prize in Physics**.

## The Idea in Plain Words

Three regions, two junctions:

| Region | Type (p-n-p) | Property | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Emitter** | p | heavily doped | supplies (emits) carriers |
| **Base** | n | **very thin**, lightly doped | the control region |
| **Collector** | p | moderately doped, large | collects the carriers |

- **Junction 1** (emitter-base) is **forward biased**, so carriers pour into the base.
- **Junction 2** (base-collector) is **reverse biased**, so its field sweeps carriers that reach it into the collector.

:::analogy
A dam with a small control gate. The emitter is a full reservoir, the base is a very thin wall with a gate, and the collector is the valley below. A small turn of the gate (a small change in emitter-base voltage) releases a huge flow into the valley. Small input, large output.
:::

## How It Works

<<SVG:callister_fig12_23>>

<<SVG:callister_fig12_24:compact>>

1. Junction 1 is forward biased, so **large numbers of holes enter the base** from the p-type emitter.
2. In the n-type base these holes are **minority carriers**. A few recombine with the base's majority electrons.
3. Because the base is **extremely narrow** and the materials are carefully prepared, **most holes sweep straight through the base** without recombining.
4. They cross the reverse-biased junction 2 and become part of the **emitter-collector current**.
5. A **small increase in the input voltage** across the emitter-base circuit therefore produces a **large increase in collector current**, which flows through the load resistor and gives a **large voltage change** across it.
6. Result: **voltage amplification**. In Figure 12.23 an input of about 0.1 mV becomes about 10 mV at the output, a gain of roughly 100.

An **n-p-n** transistor works the same way, except **electrons** (not holes) are injected across the base into the collector, and all polarities are reversed.

### Current relations (standard, not in Callister)

```text
I_E = I_B + I_C
alpha = I_C / I_E   (about 0.95 to 0.99)
beta  = I_C / I_B   (about 20 to 200)
```

Since almost all emitter current reaches the collector, I_B is a small leftover, which is why beta is large.

### Two uses

- **Amplifier**: a small signal controls a large one (above).
- **Switch**: the transistor is driven fully on or fully off, giving the 1 and 0 of digital logic. Transistors in computers act as switches and also store information.

:::key
- Two junctions back to back: **p-n-p** or **n-p-n**.
- **Emitter heavily doped, base very thin, collector collects.**
- **J1 forward, J2 reverse.**
- Carriers injected into the base are **minority carriers** there and mostly cross it without recombining.
- Small input change, large collector current change: **amplification**; on/off operation: **switching**.
:::

:::mistakes
- **"The base is thick to hold more carriers."** It is made **thin** so carriers cross it before they can recombine.
- **Biasing both junctions forward.** J2 must be **reverse** biased to collect.
- **"Holes are majority carriers in the base of a p-n-p."** The base is n-type, so injected holes are **minority** carriers there.
- **Calling a BJT voltage-controlled.** A BJT is controlled by base current; the MOSFET is the field (voltage) controlled one.
:::

:::def
A **junction (bipolar) transistor** is a three-terminal semiconductor device consisting of two p-n junctions arranged back to back (n-p-n or p-n-p): a heavily doped **emitter**, a very thin lightly doped **base** and a **collector**. With the emitter-base junction **forward biased** and the base-collector junction **reverse biased**, carriers injected from the emitter cross the thin base into the collector, so a small change in input produces a large change in output current. It is used for **amplification and switching**.
:::

## Quick Check

:::q Q1 · In a p-n-p transistor, what would happen if the base were made thick?
:::ans
Most injected holes would **recombine** with electrons in the base before reaching the collector. The collector current would fall and the transistor would lose its amplification.
:::
:::

:::q Q2 · A transistor has I_E = 10 mA and I_C = 9.9 mA. Find I_B, alpha and beta.
:::ans
**I_B = 10 - 9.9 = 0.1 mA.** **alpha = 9.9 / 10 = 0.99.** **beta = 9.9 / 0.1 = 99.**
:::
:::

:::q Q3 · Which junction is forward biased in normal amplifier operation, and why?
:::ans
**The emitter-base junction (J1)**, so that it injects carriers into the base. The base-collector junction is reverse biased so that its field collects them.
:::
:::
